Iran has shifted to a decentralized 'mosaic defense' strategy, maintaining its strike capability despite the decapitation of its top leadership. The IRGC's small-boat fleet has remained largely intact, and Iran is using cluster munitions on its remaining ballistic missiles to maximize damage despite their reduced accuracy.    Meanwhile - as I wrote earlier - Russia is providing targeting data to Iran on the positions of American warships and aircraft via its advanced reconnaissance satellite network, and China's BeiDou-3 satellite navigation system is also being utilized for more accurate missile guidance.    The Strait of Hormuz is practically closed. Iran achieved this closure not with a traditional naval blockade, but through a combination of drone strikes, missile attacks, minelaying, and – crucially – the subsequent insurance cascade. When insurers withdraw war risk coverage, ships cannot legally sail either, thus making the closure self-reinforcing, regardless of the actual level of military threat. The IRGC selectively permits passage for Chinese vessels, certain Turkish and Indian ships, as well as Iranian oil exports, demonstrating selective control over the waterway.    However, due to cyber warfare, reliable data from the region is still not reaching market analysts…   (so market cannot properly price anything… geo risk remains high)                         Subscribe          Kharg-island                 The US has destroyed over 90 military targets here (naval mine depots, missile bunkers, air defense systems). The primary reason for the military strike on Kharg Island was Iran's minelaying and drone attack offensive in the Strait of Hormuz. Iran is using its 'ghost fleets' and asymmetric naval warfare to paralyze global shipping. The US is currently employing a strategy of deterrence by punishment here. It has destroyed a portion of the Iranian Navy's offensive capacities, but has left the Kharg Island oil facilities as 'hostages' (Kharg Island handles approx. 90% of Iran's crude oil exports via four terminal complexes, which possess a daily loading capacity of 7 million barrels and a storage capacity of 30 million barrels).    If the island's loading terminals were bombed, it would shoot the price of oil above $150/barrel. The problem is not merely the loss of Iran's 1.5 million barrels per day, but the chain reaction. Due to the sustained strangulation of the Strait of Hormuz, the fate of an additional 3.5 million barrels of oil per day (mainly Iraqi and other Gulf oil) is uncertain. If Kharg falls, the sudden disappearance of supply from the market could only be briefly mitigated by the release of developed countries' Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR), and reconstruction would take anywhere from months to over a year.   LNG-crisis                I drew attention to this risk back in the first week. QatarEnergy's force majeure declaration on March 4th wiped out approx. 19% of near-term global LNG supply in a single stroke. European gas prices nearly doubled: the Dutch TTF jumped from €31.96 to €63.77/MWh. South and Southeast Asian nations face the most severe exposure: Pakistan covers 99% of its LNG needs from Qatar/UAE imports. European gas storage facilities stand at approx. 27–30% of their capacity – the lowest level since the 2022 Ukrainian energy crisis. Diesel, jet fuel, LPG, and other products will also tighten much faster.
